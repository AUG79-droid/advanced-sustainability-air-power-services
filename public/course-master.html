1.1 Sustainability is a decision property, not an aircraft label

An aircraft is not simply 'sustainable' or 'unsustainable'. Sustainability performance depends on the service delivered, the comparison being made, the period considered, the operating context and the impacts included. A decision may reduce fuel per flight hour while increasing total flying hours. It may avoid premature part replacement while increasing inspection energy. It may improve availability while requiring more inventory. The correct question is therefore not 'Is this solution sustainable?' but 'Compared with which credible alternative, what changes, for whom, over which boundary and with what evidence?'

The EASA study of the Maintenance and Production domain treats the aircraft lifecycle as a connected system involving design, production, use and maintenance, decommissioning, parts recovery, dismantling and material treatment. It also notes that fuel-burn emissions are the dominant environmental impact over an aircraft life, while maintenance, production and end-of-life remain important because they create direct impacts and influence aircraft performance, reuse and recoverability. [R1]

Key distinction: The largest climate hotspot is not automatically the only decision priority. Different impact categories can have different hotspots.

1.2 Set the boundary before collecting data

A boundary defines what is inside the assessment. A narrow operational boundary may include fuel loaded and burned during a mission. A support-system boundary may add maintenance facilities, consumables, spares, transport and ground-support equipment. A lifecycle boundary may also include the manufacture of replacement parts, retrofit embodiment, storage, retirement and end-of-life treatment. The boundary should be wide enough to capture material trade-offs and narrow enough to support the decision.

Total annual emissions are useful for organisational inventories, but they do not by themselves explain why performance changed. Intensity metrics can normalise activity but can hide growth. 1.4 From lifecycle inventory to decision-quality analysis

A lifecycle diagram is useful only when it changes the decision. The practical task is to translate a broad system into a comparison that is complete enough to capture material trade-offs and disciplined enough to avoid collecting irrelevant data. Start with the decision, not with the data that happen to be available. State the service that must be delivered, define at least two technically credible alternatives, select a common period and functional unit, and identify which flows may change because of the decision. This sequence is consistent with lifecycle thinking described in Fundamentals of Sustainable Aviation and with the EASA maintenance-and-production sustainability study. [B1][R1]

For an in-service aircraft decision, the foreground system includes processes directly controlled or changed by the option: inspections, removals, repair, replacement, facility energy, consumables, test activity, downtime and logistics. The background system includes upstream electricity, fuel, new-part production, transport services and waste treatment. The distinction is analytical rather than moral. Background flows still matter when they can reverse the apparent preference between alternatives. For example, extending a component interval may reduce planned removals but increase unscheduled transport; a digital monitoring solution may avoid interventions but add sensors, storage and validation work.

Functional units and denominators

A functional unit describes the service against which alternatives are compared. “One year” is rarely sufficient because utilisation, payload, mission class and availability may differ. Useful units include one comparable mission completed, one available aircraft-day, 1,000 flight hours within a defined mission mix, or one maintenance input achieving a specified technical outcome. No denominator is neutral. Per-flight-hour indicators can improve while absolute impact rises; per-available-aircraft-day indicators can reward unused standby capacity; per-maintenance-input comparisons can be distorted by different findings. Advanced practice therefore reports the absolute result, the intensity result and the activity driver together.

Attribution, uncertainty and double counting

A change occurring after an intervention is not automatically caused by it. Weather, mission demand, fleet mix, technical condition and reporting quality can all change the result. A credible analysis states the counterfactual: what would probably have happened without the intervention. It then separates measured values from estimates, models and assumptions. Sensitivity testing asks whether the decision changes when uncertain variables move within plausible ranges. If a one-percent change in utilisation reverses the result, the recommendation should be conditional, not categorical.

Double counting occurs when the same benefit is claimed by several actors or at several stages. A fuel reduction enabled by maintenance might appear in an operator inventory, a services programme claim and a digital-project business case. These can all describe the same physical change, but they cannot all add it independently to a corporate total. The evidence record must identify the physical outcome, reporting owner and aggregation rule.

Seven hotspot lenses

Climate is essential, but a climate-only screen can transfer burdens. Examine: (1) fuel and energy; (2) materials and critical resources; (3) water; (4) waste and hazardous substances; (5) local air emissions and noise; (6) human, safety and working-condition effects; and (7) resilience, availability and economic exposure. A hotspot is not simply the largest number. It can be a smaller flow with severe hazard, legal significance, supply vulnerability or high improvement leverage. Green Aviation and the sustainable-operations literature both stress that technology, operations and infrastructure form one system rather than independent levers. [B2][B3]

Orion worked example. The unit proposes condition-based removal for a pump. The baseline is 120 calendar removals per year; 38 units show substantial remaining condition. The proposal adds sensors and analysis, but modelling indicates 26 fewer removals, four fewer urgent shipments and two additional inspections. A responsible conclusion is not “predictive maintenance is sustainable.” It is: “Under the pilot assumptions, the option may reduce premature removals and urgent logistics while adding inspection and digital burdens; technical approval, false-negative risk and verified outcomes remain decision gates.”

Decision record

Before continuing, a reviewer should be able to answer six questions from one page: What service is required? Which alternatives are technically permitted? Which boundary and functional unit are used? Which impacts and trade-offs may be material? Which values are measured or assumed? What evidence would cause the recommendation to change? If any answer is missing, the analysis is not ready for optimisation or communication.

1.4 Diagnose hotspots using multiple lenses

A hotspot is an activity, flow or decision that makes a material contribution to an impact or creates a strong opportunity to change it. Hotspot screening should consider at least seven lenses: climate; energy and materials; water; waste and hazardous substances; noise and local pollution; people and safety; and economic resilience. Climate impact also extends beyond carbon dioxide. Aviation non-CO2 effects include nitrogen oxides, particulate matter, sulphur oxides, water vapour and effects associated with contrail-cirrus formation. Scientific uncertainty remains significant, so a simple fuel-to-CO2 calculation must not be presented as the complete climate footprint. [R2]

For Air Power Services, readiness and sustainability can reinforce one another: better technical condition can avoid inefficient operation; first-time-right maintenance can reduce repeat work; repair and controlled reuse can avoid new material; reliable forecasting can reduce emergency transport. They can also conflict: urgent aircraft-on-ground response may require air freight; redundancy can increase inventory; dispersal can duplicate equipment; and severe mission constraints can limit fuel-optimal routing. The purpose of systems thinking is to make these tensions visible rather than to force an environmental answer.

2.1 Start with the mission and remove avoidable demand

Operational efficiency begins before engine start. The first question is whether the required service can be delivered with less avoidable demand: a consolidated movement, better deployment planning, more accurate payload information, reduced repositioning, or a simulator where it genuinely meets the training objective. Demand management must never be used to understate an operational requirement; it seeks avoidable activity, not mission denial.

Once demand is established, teams can optimise the plan: payload and centre-of-gravity information, contingency fuel within approved rules, route and altitude, weather avoidance, ground power, engine run time, taxi and departure sequence. ICAO defines operations broadly, from planning and airport activity through the complete flight, and identifies operational improvements as a short- to medium-term component of its climate strategy. [R4]

2.2 Vertical and horizontal flight efficiency

Continuous Climb Operations (CCO) and Continuous Descent Operations (CDO) aim to reduce intermediate level segments and keep aircraft closer to efficient thrust and configuration. EUROCONTROL describes these techniques as enabled by airspace design, procedure design and Air Traffic Control (ATC). Its network analysis estimated substantial potential fuel, carbon and noise benefits, but also states that universal implementation is not possible because of safety, weather, capacity and controller-workload constraints. [R5]

Performance-Based Navigation (PBN) defines navigation performance requirements rather than tying every route to specific ground-based aids. Through Area Navigation (RNAV) and Required Navigation Performance (RNP) specifications, PBN can enable flexible and vertically optimised paths, reduce fuel and noise, and improve predictable access. These are capabilities, not guarantees: the realised outcome depends on procedure design, equipage, approvals, traffic management, weather and actual track flown. [R6]

2.3 Why operational efficiency is contextual

Fuel burn is produced by a chain of decisions and constraints: mission requirement, aircraft configuration and mass, technical condition, weather, route, altitude, speed, airspace structure, traffic flow, contingency policy and crew execution. Sustainable operations therefore cannot be reduced to “fly the shortest route.” The shortest geometric route may require an unsuitable level, conflict with restricted airspace, reduce weather margins or create delay. The relevant question is which authorised option delivers the required mission with the least avoidable resource use and impact.

Energy logic across a sortie

Taxi, take-off, climb, cruise, descent, approach and ground holding have different fuel drivers. Mass carried early in the flight creates a compounding effect because fuel is burned to carry fuel. Excess discretionary mass can therefore matter, but mission equipment, reserves and safety margins are not “waste.” In cruise, speed and altitude affect drag and engine efficiency; however, the optimum changes with mass, temperature, winds and operational constraints. In descent, level segments generally require additional thrust compared with an uninterrupted descent, but continuous descent operations are an opportunity subject to traffic, terrain, weather, separation and procedural availability. ICAO and EUROCONTROL present CCO/CDO as operational measures, not guaranteed savings for every flight. [R4][R5]

Performance-Based Navigation allows aircraft to fly paths based on defined navigation performance rather than a fixed sequence of ground beacons. This can enable repeatable tracks, optimised arrivals and improved airspace design. It does not itself create a benefit: the procedure must be designed, approved, available and flown, and system-level effects must be assessed. A route that saves fuel for one aircraft but increases vectoring or congestion elsewhere may shift rather than eliminate impact.

Direct CO₂, lifecycle GHG and non-CO₂ effects

Multiplying Jet-A/Jet-A1 fuel by 3.16 gives an estimate of direct combustion CO₂. It is useful because it is transparent and directly linked to fuel. It is not a complete lifecycle greenhouse-gas result: extraction, refining, transport and fuel production are outside the combustion boundary. Nor is it a complete climate result. Aviation also affects climate through nitrogen oxides, water vapour, particles and contrail-cirrus processes, with effects that depend on altitude, location, time and atmospheric conditions. The European Aviation Environmental Report explains why CO₂ and non-CO₂ effects should be distinguished rather than collapsed into an unsupported single claim. [R2]

State-aircraft applicability

Civil aviation policy is valuable evidence but not automatically a legal requirement for State aircraft. CORSIA, EU ETS aviation and ReFuelEU Aviation have defined scopes, exemptions and accountable entities. A defence or military operation must be checked against the exact legal text, national implementation, contract and customer requirements. Even where a civil rule does not apply, its methods may still provide a benchmark. The course therefore separates three statements: “legally applicable,” “methodologically useful,” and “aligned with a broader policy direction.” Confusing them creates compliance risk and greenwashing.

Operational trade-offs and rebound

An efficiency improvement can lower fuel per mission while total fuel rises because activity increases. This is not evidence that efficiency failed; it means absolute and intensity performance answer different questions. Faster turnaround can reduce auxiliary-power use but increase vehicle movements. A route optimisation can reduce fuel while changing noise distribution. Lower contingency fuel may reduce mass but is never an environmental decision outside approved planning rules. The operational gate remains safety, airworthiness, security and authorised mission requirements.

Orion worked example. For 310 comparable training sorties, an approved routing change saves an estimated 85 kg of fuel per sortie. Direct combustion CO₂ avoided is 85 × 310 × 3.16 ÷ 1,000 = 83.3 tonnes. The evidence statement must add the baseline period, mission class, method used to estimate fuel, weather and payload controls, and whether the change caused delay or additional activity elsewhere. The claim must say “estimated direct combustion CO₂,” not “total emissions.”

A defensible operational improvement cycle

  1. Define comparable mission classes and exclusions.
  2. Establish a baseline using fuel, time, route and contextual variables.
  3. Select only authorised levers and document constraints.
  4. Run a bounded trial with a counterfactual or matched comparison.
  5. Report absolute fuel, intensity, activity and uncertainty.
  6. Check noise, delay, readiness and other trade-offs.
  7. Standardise the measure only after repeatable evidence.

This sequence reflects the systems approach described in sustainable aviation and air-traffic-management literature rather than treating a single flight-planning feature as a universal solution. [B1][B4]

2.3 Measure what the operation actually changed

Fuel is a strong operational indicator because it links directly to cost and combustion carbon dioxide. For Jet-A and Jet-A1, ICAO uses a fuel conversion factor of 3.16 kilograms of CO2 per kilogram of fuel in CORSIA calculations. [R20] This is a tank-to-wake combustion factor. It is not a complete lifecycle carbon footprint and does not include aviation non-CO2 climate effects.

Worked calculation: If an approved planning change saves 120 kg of Jet-A1 per mission, direct combustion CO2 avoided is 120 x 3.16 = 379.2 kg CO2 per mission. At 240 comparable missions, the estimated direct effect is 91.0 t CO2. Report the factor, mission count and comparison conditions.

Noise and local air quality require different metrics. Fuel savings cannot be assumed to reduce noise at every location; a route change can move exposure between communities. A higher flight path may reduce local noise while changing fuel. Measures should therefore be evaluated against the material impact and affected stakeholder rather than compressed into one unsupported score.

2.4 Mission readiness can change the optimal answer

Commercial efficiency logic cannot simply be copied into state or military aviation. A mission may require a time window, threat-avoidance route, diplomatic clearance, training objective, reserve policy or rapid re-tasking capacity. An apparently inefficient route can be necessary. The analytical task is to distinguish required constraints from avoidable inefficiency and to document why the chosen option remains the best permitted solution.

3.1 Maintenance is both a footprint and an enabler

Maintenance consumes energy, water, parts, chemicals, consumables, packaging and labour; it also produces wastewater, emissions and waste. At the same time, maintenance preserves airworthiness, reliability and technical condition. A well-maintained aircraft can avoid performance deterioration, repeat defects, premature removals and emergency logistics. Sustainable maintenance therefore cannot mean 'less maintenance'. It means the right approved task, at the right time, with the right resources, producing the intended technical outcome.

EASA's M&P study highlights maintenance impacts from energy use, maintenance products, spare parts and waste, and notes that time pressure, safety and reliability dominate the MRO environment. 3.3 Maintenance as both footprint and performance enabler

Maintenance has two environmental roles. It creates a direct footprint through facilities, energy, water, chemicals, consumables, parts, packaging, waste and transport. It also changes the performance of the aircraft and support system by maintaining aerodynamic condition, engine efficiency, reliability, availability and component life. These roles must be measured separately before they are connected. Otherwise a team may exaggerate a small direct improvement as a fleet-level climate benefit or overlook a facility hotspot because flight fuel is larger.

Follow the physical work

A useful MRO assessment begins at the work order and walks the real process: receipt and staging; access; cleaning and preparation; inspection and non-destructive testing; removal; repair or replacement; surface treatment; reassembly; testing; documentation; release; packaging and dispatch. At each step record inputs, outputs, abnormal losses, repeat work and decision points. Procurement data show what was bought, not necessarily where it was used or lost. Waste data show what left the process, not the avoided material or the reason it became waste.

The waste hierarchy is a decision hierarchy

Prevention generally preserves more value than recycling. In MRO, prevention can mean avoiding an unnecessary removal, reducing over-ordering, eliminating repeat defects, extending shelf life or substituting a process after technical approval. Preparation for reuse and repair can preserve component function, but aviation traceability and approved status are non-negotiable. Recycling is appropriate when function cannot safely be retained; energy recovery and disposal are lower-value routes. A high recycling rate can coexist with growing total waste, so both generation and treatment-route data are needed. The EU Waste Framework Directive supplies the hierarchy, while aviation-specific application requires approved technical and quality controls. [R21]

Hazardous substances and substitution

A lower-VOC or bio-based product is not automatically preferable. The comparison must consider hazard classification, worker exposure, process temperature, drying energy, quantity used, compatibility, corrosion, surface quality, waste route and emergency controls. Substitution must follow the approved technical-change process and applicable chemical, occupational and environmental requirements. A product-level attribute cannot substitute for a process-level assessment. The correct order is: define function; screen hazards; verify technical compatibility; compare total process effects; pilot under controlled conditions; approve; then monitor.

First-time-right and failure demand

Repeat work consumes labour, energy, material and capacity without delivering additional service. It may arise from unclear instructions, access damage, contamination, tool availability, incomplete information, late findings or supply defects. Treating repeat work only as waste misses its operational significance. A combined measure might track repeat operations per maintenance input, material discarded because of rework, and delay created. Root-cause action can then improve quality, environmental performance and readiness together.

Orion worked example. A hangar walkdown finds conditioned air escaping through open doors, compressed-air leaks, solvent cleaning repeated after contamination, mixed waste and lighting operating outside work periods. The poster colour is irrelevant. The team should quantify duration, demand and affected work, then prioritise actions by controllability, environmental significance, technical risk and payback. Closing doors may be simple; changing cleaning chemistry requires approval; changing inspection frequency requires an airworthiness case.

Balanced MRO metrics

No single KPI is sufficient. Pair environmental performance with technical guardrails: kWh per comparable maintenance input plus first-pass yield; hazardous waste per work package plus defect rate; premature removals per 1,000 flight hours plus reliability; urgent shipments plus aircraft-on-ground events; water per cleaning operation plus cleanliness acceptance. Use absolute totals to manage site objectives and normalised indicators to diagnose process performance. Define owner, data source, frequency, exclusions and review trigger for every metric.

The sustainable aircraft-maintenance literature emphasises lifecycle cost, reliability, material choice, process optimisation and end-of-life value. The strongest application in Air Power Services is not a label such as “green MRO,” but a controlled improvement system that prevents unnecessary physical work while protecting the approved technical outcome. 4.3 From prediction to verified physical outcome

A dashboard, algorithm or digital twin has no environmental benefit by itself. Benefit arises only when information changes an authorised decision, that decision changes physical action, and the action produces a measurable outcome compared with a credible counterfactual. The causal chain is: data → model → decision → authorised action → physical outcome → governed claim. Breaking any link turns a sustainability claim into an assumption.

Maintenance strategies are defined by decision logic

Preventive maintenance uses predetermined intervals; condition-based maintenance responds to observed condition; predictive maintenance estimates future condition or remaining useful life; prescriptive systems recommend an action under defined objectives and constraints. The same sensor can support several strategies. The distinction lies in how information triggers a decision, who authorises it and what technical basis applies. Predictive output never overrides approved maintenance data or delegated authority.

Counterfactual and benefit ledger

A digital pilot must specify what would happen without it. A calendar baseline, historical average or untreated comparison group may be suitable, but each carries bias. Record the expected causal pathway and classify metrics. Activity metrics include alerts, users and reports. Decision metrics include recommendations accepted or work orders changed. Physical outcome metrics include removals avoided, parts retained, emergency shipments reduced, downtime changed, fuel saved or waste prevented. Environmental outcome metrics translate physical change using an explicit method and boundary. The ledger must also record added sensors, computing, storage, travel, false alerts and rebound.

Model risk and assurance

False positives create unnecessary inspections, removals or spares demand. False negatives can miss degradation and carry safety and availability consequences. Performance must therefore be evaluated with cost-sensitive measures, not accuracy alone. Data drift, sensor failure, maintenance-induced changes and fleet heterogeneity can degrade a model after deployment. Human factors matter: an opaque recommendation may be ignored, while automation bias may produce over-reliance. EASA’s human-centric AI roadmap and aviation predictive-maintenance research highlight data quality, explainability, assurance, certification and organisational adoption barriers. [R12][R13][R14]

Digital footprint and rebound

Computing impact depends on hardware, utilisation, location, electricity mix, model size, data retention and system architecture. In many maintenance cases the digital footprint may be small relative to avoided physical activity, but it should be measured rather than assumed. Rebound occurs if easier monitoring expands data collection without decision value, if avoided downtime increases activity, or if additional alerts create more work. A pilot should therefore define a retention policy, minimum useful data, review trigger and retirement criterion.

Orion worked example. A component-health dashboard gains 70 users and produces 2,000 alerts. These are not sustainability outcomes. During the pilot, ten urgent removals and six emergency shipments are avoided, while data storage rises 15% and three false alerts produce inspections. The claim can address verified operational changes; emissions must be calculated from actual shipment mode, distance, mass and method. The false-alert burden and digital inputs remain in the balance.

5.3 Compare extension, retrofit, replacement and retirement on equal terms

“Keep the old aircraft” and “replace it with a more efficient aircraft” are both incomplete positions. Life extension preserves embodied value and delays new production, but may retain higher fuel burn, obsolescence and maintenance burden. Replacement can improve in-service efficiency and capability but creates production, infrastructure, training and transition impacts. Retrofit lies between them: it consumes material, downtime and engineering effort now in exchange for expected performance over remaining service. The correct option depends on service demand, utilisation, remaining life, technical feasibility and system consequences.

Scenario structure

All options must deliver the same defined service over the same decision horizon. Build at least four scenarios: continue or extend with required maintenance; retrofit and operate; replace and transition; retire without replacement where service demand can genuinely be removed or met differently. Include embodiment, installation, infrastructure, training, maintenance, spares, fuel, downtime, reliability, logistics, storage and end-of-life. Do not credit avoided production if another asset must be produced elsewhere to deliver the service.

Break-even reasoning

A retrofit has an upfront environmental investment and an annual operational effect. A simple break-even time divides embodiment impact by annual saving, but the result is only a screen. Annual saving varies with utilisation, mission mix, degradation and achieved performance. Discounting may be used for financial cash flows; environmental results should show physical quantities by year rather than hiding timing assumptions. If remaining service life is shorter than break-even, the retrofit may not deliver the expected benefit. If utilisation is uncertain, show low, central and high cases.

Circular value follows function and information

The highest-value route generally retains function: continued authorised use, repair, overhaul or reuse as an approved spare. If aerospace function cannot be preserved, alternative use may retain form; material recovery retains less value; energy recovery and disposal are lower routes. Traceability is a material property in aviation economics: without records, a physically serviceable part may have little or no approved reuse value. End-of-life planning should therefore start before retirement by maintaining configuration, material and maintenance records and defining ownership.

EASA notes that serviceable components may return as approved spares and that general waste legislation applies once aircraft and parts enter waste status. It also distinguishes reuse, alternative use, recycling, recovery and landfill. These routes must not be merged into a single “recycled” percentage. Composite structures, hazardous materials and mixed assemblies require specific treatment and can constrain recovery. [R15][R21]

Stocks, cannibalisation and obsolescence

Parts harvesting can support ageing fleets and avoid new production, but it is not automatically circular. It requires approved status, records, preservation, inspection, ownership controls and a credible demand. Uncontrolled harvesting can create stranded inventory, duplicate storage and waste. Obsolescence management should integrate demand forecasting, repair capability, last-time buys, shelf life and disposal planning. The aim is to preserve availability with the lowest whole-system burden, not maximise stock or minimise it in isolation.

Orion worked example. A modification saves 1.5% of 6,500 kg on 420 missions per year: 40.95 tonnes of fuel annually under the central case. Before approval, Orion must compare embodiment material and downtime, remaining life, mission variability, maintenance change and substitute capacity. At 180 missions per year, the break-even may move beyond remaining service; at surge utilisation, it may shorten. The recommendation should state the utilisation threshold at which preference changes.

Retirement decision record

A controlled retirement plan identifies the legal owner, final decommissioning point, hazardous materials, approved parts recovery, records, waste classification, contractors, destination evidence and performance reporting. Mass sent to a contractor is not proof of recycling. Evidence should distinguish prepared for reuse, recycled, recovered, disposed and pending. Claims such as “zero waste aircraft” require a clear denominator, exclusions and independently supportable destination data.

Lifecycle and circular-economy literature converge on a practical conclusion: age alone does not decide sustainability. The decision rests on comparable service, future utilisation, embodied and operational effects, system readiness and evidence quality. [B1][B6]

5.2 Extend, replace, store or retire: compare the same service

An older aircraft can have higher fuel and maintenance needs but avoids immediate production of a replacement. A new aircraft can offer better operational performance but carries manufacturing, delivery, infrastructure and training impacts. Life extension can preserve embodied value but may increase obsolescence, scarcity, downtime or urgent logistics. There is no universal rule that replacement or extension is environmentally preferable.

The scenarios must deliver the same defined service over the same horizon. If a replacement provides different capability or availability, that difference must be made explicit. A break-even analysis estimates when cumulative operational savings exceed the additional embodied and transition impacts, then tests sensitivity to utilisation, fuel saving, maintenance, energy mix and service life.

5.3 End-of-life begins before dismantling

Aircraft can move from operation to parking, storage, decommissioning, disassembly/part-out, dismantling and material treatment. Decommissioning removes the aircraft from its operational status and prepares it safely. Disassembly recovers identified parts and materials, with status, records, inspection and recertification where applicable. Dismantling separates the remaining structure and materials for recycling, recovery or disposal.

EASA's M&P study describes hazardous-fluid removal, decontamination, parts identification and packaging, material sorting, recycling, energy recovery and landfill. It also notes that aircraft end-of-life is not governed by one dedicated worldwide aircraft law; general waste and hazardous-material rules apply when assets become waste. [R1] EASA highlights eco-design, disassembly, reuse and recyclability as important future improvements. [R15]

Sequence Control objective
1. Status and decision Document ownership, configuration, airworthiness and retirement decision
2. Make safe Defuel, depressurise, isolate energy and control hazardous materials
3. Records and parts plan Identify reusable/serviceable candidates and required traceability
4. Disassembly/part-out Remove, inspect, tag, package and route parts correctly
5. Dismantling and sorting Separate materials and hazardous/non-recyclable fractions
6. Treatment Prioritise reuse/recycling, then recovery, then compliant disposal
7. Evidence closure Mass balance, certificates, waste records and lessons for design/support

Circularity is constrained by technical status and material reality. A component cannot return to service because it is physically reusable; it must have the required identity, history, inspection and release. Composite materials can be technically difficult or economically unattractive to recycle. Manufacturer statements such as 'up to' recovery percentages are context-specific and must not be generalised to every aircraft, configuration or treatment route. [R16]

5.4 Defence circularity and strategic resilience

Circularity in defence can also support resilience and strategic autonomy through repair, remanufacturing, reverse logistics, spare-parts management and reduced dependence on critical materials. The European Defence Agency's IF CEED brings governments, industry and research organisations together on these topics. [R11] The opportunity must still be tested against certification, configuration, security, intellectual property and supply continuity.

6.1 Governance is a layered system

A framework can be a global aspiration, a legal obligation, a technical standard, a voluntary method, a customer requirement or an internal objective. These categories are not interchangeable. ICAO's Long-Term Global Aspirational Goal (LTAG) is net-zero carbon emissions from international aviation by 2050, but ICAO states that the LTAG does not assign a specific emissions target to an individual State. [R3]

Civil aviation schemes also have defined scope. ICAO's CORSIA monitoring, reporting and verification requirements apply to international civil aviation and exclude police, military, customs and state aircraft flights. [R7] ReFuelEU Aviation applies to defined civil commercial air transport and states that military aircraft and several special operations are outside its scope. [R8] These frameworks may still offer useful concepts, factors or market context, but they must not be described as directly binding on Air Power operations without an applicability check.

6.3 Turn frameworks into controlled decisions

A list of acronyms is not governance. Governance identifies which requirement or commitment applies, to whom, for which activity, with what evidence, owner, control and escalation route. In Air Power Services, the same topic may be influenced by site environmental law, customer contracts, airworthiness rules, corporate objectives, voluntary standards, defence methodologies and civil aviation policy. Their authority and scope are different. A strong roadmap never presents a voluntary method as law or a civil scheme as automatically binding on military operations.

Applicability hierarchy

  1. Mandatory gates: safety, airworthiness, security, export control and authorised mission requirements.
  2. Legal and permit requirements: site, waste, chemicals, emissions, energy or other obligations confirmed in the legal register.
  3. Contract and customer requirements: programme-specific commitments and data rules.
  4. Corporate governance: policies, objectives, accounting rules and approval processes.
  5. Standards and methods: ISO 14001, lifecycle methods, NATO measurement approaches and technical guidance.
  6. Contextual policy: civil aviation goals and schemes used only within their defined scope or as methodological benchmarks.

ICAO’s long-term aspirational goal concerns international civil aviation. CORSIA has defined monitoring, reporting and offsetting scope. ReFuelEU Aviation establishes obligations for specified aviation-fuel supply and airport contexts. NATO climate work connects mitigation and adaptation with operational effectiveness. These instruments can inform the same roadmap, but they cannot be treated as interchangeable obligations. [R3][R7][R8][R9][R10]

KPI governance

Every KPI needs a definition sheet: purpose; owner; physical boundary; organisational boundary; unit; formula; data source; frequency; baseline; exclusions; estimation method; quality checks; restatement rule; target; review trigger and claim limitations. Without this sheet, two teams can report the same label with different meanings. A percentage reduction is incomplete without baseline year, baseline value, scope and activity context. An intensity target must be paired with absolute performance so growth is visible.

Targets and roadmap logic

A roadmap should connect problem, action, output, outcome and strategic contribution. “Deploy a dashboard” is an output; “reduce emergency shipments for the selected component family while maintaining availability” is an outcome. Assign an accountable owner with decision authority, not a broad department. Define a near-term milestone, outcome indicator, technical guardrail and stop/review trigger. Portfolio selection should consider impact, evidence strength, controllability, cost, time, risk, dependencies and learning value.

Claims control and greenwashing prevention

A claim must be no stronger than the evidence. Avoid absolute labels such as green, zero-impact or sustainable aircraft. State the subject, baseline, period, boundary, method, result and material limitation. Distinguish achieved results from forecasts and targets. Separate direct combustion CO₂ from lifecycle GHG and total climate effects. Do not imply legal compliance from voluntary alignment or corporate contribution from an unverified pilot. ISO 14001 supports controlled objectives, monitoring and improvement, but certification does not prove that a specific product or service has a lower impact. [R22]

Claim repair. Replace “Our digital twin cut fleet emissions by 25%” with: “During the defined pilot, the digital planning process reduced emergency shipments for the selected component family by 25% against the stated baseline. This result does not represent total fleet emissions; associated logistics CO₂e will be calculated from verified shipment data using the documented method.” The repaired version narrows the subject, distinguishes activity from emissions and discloses the next evidence step.

Assurance and review

Evidence should be proportionate to the decision and claim. Internal operational decisions may use transparent estimates with uncertainty. External or high-profile claims require stronger review, traceable data and approved methods. Establish three lines: process owners create and retain evidence; environmental, quality, legal or technical functions challenge method and applicability; independent audit tests controls and records. Correct errors through a documented restatement process rather than silently changing baselines.

24-month implementation rhythm

Months 0–3: confirm scope, owners, baselines and legal/contract applicability. Months 4–9: run bounded pilots and repair data gaps. Months 10–15: verify physical outcomes, trade-offs and guardrails. Months 16–21: scale only measures with repeatable evidence. Months 22–24: review portfolio, restate where necessary and publish bounded claims. NATO and defence circular-economy initiatives reinforce the value of measurement, resilience and controlled resource use without subordinating operational effectiveness. [R9][R10][R11]

The final test is simple: can another competent reviewer reproduce the KPI, understand why the framework applies, trace the evidence and see the limitation without relying on marketing language? If not, the roadmap is not yet governed.

6.3 Control sustainability claims

A claim should state the action, result, boundary, period, baseline, method and limitations in language proportionate to the evidence. Avoid vague terms such as green, eco-friendly, clean, zero-impact or sustainable without an explained and substantiated meaning. Glossary

Absolute impact. Total impact over a defined period or boundary, not divided by activity.

Aircraft-on-ground (AOG). A condition in which an aircraft cannot return to service until required action is completed.

Air Traffic Control (ATC). Service that manages aircraft movement and separation in controlled airspace and at aerodromes.

Air Traffic Management (ATM). The wider system for managing air traffic and airspace safely and efficiently.

Baseline. The defined reference against which change is assessed.

Carbon dioxide equivalent (CO2e). A common unit expressing different greenhouse gases according to a stated global-warming metric.

Condition-based maintenance. Maintenance decisions triggered by evidence of asset condition within an approved process.

Continuous Climb Operations (CCO). Climb technique that minimises intermediate level segments where conditions permit.

Continuous Descent Operations (CDO). Descent technique that minimises level segments and unnecessary thrust where conditions permit.

Carbon Offsetting and Reduction Scheme for International Aviation (CORSIA). ICAO scheme for international civil aviation with defined monitoring, reporting, verification and offsetting scope.

Counterfactual. The credible alternative scenario used to estimate what would have happened without the intervention.

Digital twin. A data-connected digital representation of a physical asset, process or system used for analysis or decision support.

Direct combustion CO2. Carbon dioxide produced by fuel combustion; it is not the complete lifecycle or non-CO2 climate impact.

End-of-life (EoL). Activities from retirement/decommissioning through parts recovery, dismantling, material treatment and disposal.

Environmental Management System (EMS). A structured system for managing environmental aspects, obligations, controls, objectives and improvement.

Functional unit. The quantified reference service used to compare alternatives.

Greenwashing. A misleading impression that an organisation, product, service or action has better environmental performance than evidence supports.

Hotspot. A material source of impact or a strong leverage point within the defined system.

Intensity indicator. Impact divided by a unit of activity or service.

International Civil Aviation Organization (ICAO). United Nations specialised agency that develops global civil aviation standards, policies and guidance.

Life Cycle Assessment (LCA). Method for evaluating environmental impacts across defined lifecycle stages and a specified functional unit.

Long-Term Global Aspirational Goal (LTAG). ICAO goal of net-zero carbon emissions from international aviation by 2050; not an individual State target.

Maintenance, Repair and Overhaul (MRO). Organisations and activities that inspect, maintain, repair and overhaul aircraft, engines, components and systems.

Mission readiness. The ability to deliver the authorised mission or service at the required time and performance.

Non-CO2 effects. Climate effects associated with aviation emissions and processes other than carbon dioxide, including NOx, particles, water vapour and contrail-related effects.

Performance-Based Navigation (PBN). Navigation based on specified performance requirements, including RNAV and RNP applications.

Predictive maintenance. Use of data and models to forecast future condition, failure probability or remaining useful life.

Prescriptive maintenance. Decision support that recommends a maintenance action or schedule under defined objectives and constraints.

Rebound effect. A benefit that is partly or fully offset by increased activity, new resource demand or unintended behaviour.

ReFuelEU Aviation. European Union regulation for sustainable aviation fuel supply and use in defined civil-commercial scope; military aircraft are outside that scope.

Reliability. Probability that an item performs its required function under stated conditions for a stated period.

Remaining useful life (RUL). Estimated time or usage before an asset reaches a defined condition or limit.

Required Navigation Performance (RNP). PBN navigation specification that includes onboard performance monitoring and alerting.

Safety Data Sheet (SDS). Document describing hazards, handling, controls and emergency information for a chemical product.

Sustainable Aviation Fuel (SAF). Aviation fuel meeting a defined sustainability and lifecycle methodology; benefits depend on pathway, accounting and scope.

System boundary. The processes, organisations, lifecycle stages and flows included in an assessment.

Volatile Organic Compound (VOC). Organic compound that can evaporate and contribute to air emissions; regulatory definitions vary.

Waste hierarchy. Priority order of prevention, preparation for reuse, recycling, other recovery and disposal.

Research notes applied to the course

  • Fuel burn is presented as a dominant lifecycle climate driver without claiming that it is the hotspot for every environmental category. [R1][R2]

  • EUROCONTROL CCO/CDO benefits are presented as network potential with explicit operational constraints, not guaranteed savings. [R5]

  • The ICAO 3.16 factor is labelled as direct combustion CO2 for Jet-A/Jet-A1, not a full lifecycle or total climate factor. [R20]

  • Predictive-maintenance claims require evidence from decision and outcome, reflecting documented aviation adoption barriers. [R12][R13]

  • CORSIA and ReFuelEU scope distinctions prevent civil requirements from being incorrectly imposed on military/state operations. [R7][R8]

  • End-of-life recovery is separated into reuse, recycling, recovery and disposal; context-specific percentages are not generalised. [R1][R15][R16]

  • Defence sustainability is connected to operational effectiveness, resilience and controlled measurement through NATO and EDA sources. [R9][R10][R11]
